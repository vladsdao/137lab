# The science under the vibe

*Status: research notes. Everything here is either an external published source or an explicitly open question. We separate the two on purpose.*

## What is documented

**Synchronous movement bonds strangers.** Moving in time with other people measurably raises trust and cooperation between them — even when the movement is as plain as walking or tapping (Wiltermuth & Heath, *Synchrony and Cooperation*, Psychological Science, 2009). Historian William McNeill called the phenomenon **muscular bonding** and traced it through drill, dance and ritual (*Keeping Together in Time*, Harvard University Press, 1995). Sociology has its own name for the heat of a crowd moving as one: Durkheim's *collective effervescence*.

**Synchronization without a conductor is normal physics.** Fireflies flashing together, pendulum clocks on one beam, cardiac pacemaker cells — coupled oscillators fall into step with no one in charge. The standard mathematics is the **Kuramoto model**; its **order parameter r ∈ [0, 1]** measures how aligned the ensemble's phases are: r = 0 is noise, r = 1 is perfect unison.

This is why *r* is our honest metric of "how much are we one": it is not a vibe-word, it is the same number physicists put on fireflies.

**Our metaphor-engine is fireflies, not entanglement.** We deliberately do not reach for quantum entanglement to explain the game: emergent synchronization of coupled oscillators is enough, is real, and is checkable.

## What is NOT documented — the open question

Every bonding study above puts bodies **in one room**: they share air, sound, peripheral vision. The Synchronizator removes the room.

> **Does the bonding effect of synchrony survive distance — when the only thing people share is the moment itself?**

We don't know. Nobody seems to know. The game is the instrument: r-curves of distributed circles, the words people leave after a wave, whether they return. If the effect dies with distance, we will have measured that honestly — and that answer is worth having too.
